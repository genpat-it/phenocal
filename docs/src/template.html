phenocal

How to compare a phenotype measured by different laboratories, using genetically identical isolates as built-in reference samples.

1The problem

A phenotype such as a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is often collected from several studies or laboratories, each with its own protocol. Protocols differ in medium, inoculum or reading, and these differences shift the whole scale: one laboratory may read every strain two dilutions higher than another. Pooling the raw values then mixes biology with protocol. A classifier trained on the pooled data learns which laboratory produced a value, and a threshold such as "tolerant if MIC ≥ 1.25 mg/L" means something different in each cohort.

The usual way to fix the scale is a shared reference: the same control strains measured by every laboratory. When the data come from published studies, such controls rarely exist. phenocal finds them in the data.

2The idea: natural controls

On the logarithmic scale, the measured value of isolate \(i\) in cohort \(c\) is modelled as its biology plus a cohort offset plus noise:

$$ y_{i} = \log_{10}(\text{value}_i) = \mu_i + \delta_{c(i)} + \varepsilon_i . $$

The offset \(\delta_c\) is multiplicative on the raw scale (a factor \(10^{\delta_c}\)), which is how protocol differences such as a dilution shift behave. If two isolates measured in different cohorts have the same genome, they have the same biology, \(\mu_i = \mu_j\), and the biology cancels in their difference.

Large surveillance collections contain many such pairs, because the same clones circulate widely and are sampled again and again. Each pair gives a noisy estimate of \(\delta_B - \delta_A\); many pairs give a precise one. The genome, read through cgMLST or wgMLST allelic distances, plays the role of the reference sample.

3The algorithm at a glance

@@FLOW@@

3.1Cross-cohort pairs

The inputs are two TSV files: the phenotypes (sample, value, cohort) and the genomic distances, either as a pair table (sample_i, sample_j, distance) or as a distance matrix straight from chewBBACA, ReporTree or cgmlst-dists. phenocal keeps the pairs whose two isolates belong to different cohorts. For each, it records the genomic distance \(d\) and the log-ratio

$$ s = y_i - y_j \qquad (i \text{ in cohort } a,\; j \text{ in cohort } b). $$

Pairs within one cohort carry no information on the offsets and are ignored.

3.2Which pairs count: the adaptive bound τ

Isolates at distance 0 are the cleanest controls, but there may be too few of them. Isolates a few alleles apart are almost as good, while distant isolates differ in biology and would bias the estimate. For every pair of cohorts, phenocal sorts the pairs by distance and picks the bound \(\tau\) automatically: the smallest distance at which there are at least --min-support pairs with \(d \le \tau\) and the median of their log-ratios has not drifted from the median at the smallest distance by more than --max-drift units of measurement resolution. Taking the smallest such bound keeps the evidence as close to identical genomes as the data allow. If no bound satisfies both conditions, all pairs of that cohort pair are used, and the large τ and small number of pairs give the edge a low weight.

The bound is chosen separately for every pair of cohorts, so there is no global distance threshold to tune, and it works the same with cgMLST and wgMLST distances, which live on different scales.

3.3One number per cohort pair

The edge value is the median log-ratio of the pairs within the bound, a robust estimate of the offset difference:

$$ \Delta_{ab} = \operatorname{median}\{\, s : d \le \tau_{ab} \,\} \;\approx\; \delta_a - \delta_b . $$

Its standard error combines the measurement resolution of the two cohorts \(\sigma_a, \sigma_b\), a term for the biology that can still differ between isolates up to distance \(\tau\), and the number of pairs \(n_{ab}\):

$$ \mathrm{SE}_{ab} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_a^2 + \sigma_b^2 + \lambda\,\tau_{ab}}{n_{ab}}}, \qquad w_{ab} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{SE}_{ab}^2}. $$

By default \(\sigma_c = \log_{10} 2 / \sqrt 2\), the resolution of a two-fold dilution series, and \(\lambda = (\log_{10} 2)^2\); --sigma empirical estimates each \(\sigma_c\) from the cohort's own measurement grid instead.

3.4Solving the graph

Cohorts are the nodes of a comparison graph and every pair of cohorts with supporting pairs is an edge. Each edge says how far apart its two cohorts are; the offsets are the node values that agree best with all edges at once, by weighted least squares:

$$ \hat\delta = \arg\min_{\delta}\; \sum_{(a,b)} w_{ab}\,\big(\delta_a - \delta_b - \Delta_{ab}\big)^2 \quad \text{with } \delta_{\text{anchor}} = 0 . $$

Only differences of offsets are identified, so one cohort, the anchor, is fixed at 0 (--anchor, by default the largest cohort). The anchor is a choice of units, like choosing which laboratory's scale to report in: changing it shifts all harmonised values by the same factor and leaves every comparison between isolates unchanged. The graph must be connected: a cohort with no genetic link to the others cannot be calibrated, and phenocal says so. Cohorts linked only through other cohorts are fine: labC can be calibrated against labA through labB.

3.5Uncertainty

Every edge is uncertain, so the offsets are too. phenocal draws each edge value from \(\mathcal N(\Delta_{ab}, \mathrm{SE}_{ab}^2)\), solves the graph again, and repeats this --bootstrap times (10,000 by default). The reported offset is the median of the draws and the 95% credible interval spans their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. --robust draws from a Student-t instead of a normal.

Near-identical pairs are often nested in clonal clusters, and pairs from the same cluster are not independent. With --clusters (a table sample → cluster), phenocal also reports a cluster-weighted estimate and an interval that resamples whole clusters across the graph, the preferred summary when cluster ids are available.

3.6Harmonised values and probabilistic labels

The harmonised value of an isolate is its raw value brought to the anchor's scale, \(\text{value} / 10^{\hat\delta_c}\). Because \(\hat\delta_c\) is uncertain, so is the label of an isolate near a threshold. For each threshold \(c\) of --thresholds, phenocal reports the probability that the harmonised value is at least \(c\), over the uncertainty of the cohort offset:

$$ P_i(c) = \Pr\!\big( y_i - \delta_{c(i)} \ge \log_{10} c \big), \qquad \delta_{c(i)} \sim \mathcal N\big(\hat\delta_{c(i)},\, s_{c(i)}^2\big), $$

with \(s_c\) the half-width of the 95% interval divided by 1.96.

Instead of a hard label that may flip with a small change in the offset, a downstream model can use these probabilities, or drop the isolates whose label is uncertain. Finally, phenocal estimates a data-driven cutoff on the harmonised scale with two methods that bracket it: the antimode of a kernel density estimate and the crossover of a two-component Gaussian mixture, with a bootstrap interval.

4Why it matters for machine learning

phenocal is a pre-processing step for supervised learning on pooled cohorts: it fixes the target, the phenotype, before any model sees it. Its inputs are the phenotypes, the cohort labels and the genomic distances, never the model's features or the model itself, so it can sit in front of any classifier or regressor.

@@MLFLOW@@

4.1Classifiers

A classifier needs a label, usually obtained by cutting the phenotype at a threshold (for example tolerant if MIC ≥ 1.25 mg/L). With raw values, a cohort whose protocol reads high falls entirely above the threshold and a cohort that reads low falls mostly below it. Two things then go wrong:

  • The model learns the cohort. Cohorts usually sample different populations, so the genome tells which cohort an isolate comes from. A classifier trained on pooled raw labels can look accurate by recognising the cohort rather than the trait, and nothing in a random cross-validation reveals it.
  • The honest test is impossible. The real question is whether the model transfers to cohorts it was not trained on. If the held-out cohorts contain a single class, balanced accuracy and ROC-AUC are undefined: the endpoint cannot be evaluated at all.

On that restored endpoint the paper reports, for a gradient-boosted tree classifier on allelic profiles, a forward ROC-AUC of 0.999 with wgMLST features and 0.748 with cgMLST features, and 0.880 in the reverse direction. The endpoint stays evaluable across the pre-declared thresholds 0.6–2.0 mg/L, with the AUC moving only mildly with the cutoff, and the result is not leakage: it falls to chance with permuted training labels and with a cohort-only baseline (AUC 0.50), and is unchanged when the isolates used as calibration twins are removed from training. These values show that the endpoint has become evaluable; they are not proof that the calibrated MICs are the biological truth.

The probabilistic labels \(P_i(c)\) give two more options beyond the hard label: use them as soft targets or sample weights, or train only on isolates whose label is stable (for example \(P_i(c) < 0.1\) or \(> 0.9\)) and leave the uncertain ones out. Isolates of the anchor cohort always get 0 or 1; in other cohorts the probability departs from 0 or 1 only near the threshold, and more so the less certain the cohort offset.

4.2Regressors

To predict the phenotype itself, for example the MIC rather than a tolerant/sensitive call, the target is the log value \(y_i = \mu_i + \delta_{c(i)} + \varepsilon_i\). Trained on raw values, a regressor has to explain the offsets \(\delta_c\) too, and because cohort and genome are correlated it attributes them to the genome. Its predictions then mix biology with protocol, are on no particular laboratory's scale, and carry the offset of whichever cohort a new isolate resembles; prediction errors measured across cohorts are inflated by the offsets rather than reflecting the biology.

Trained on the harmonised log values \(y_i - \hat\delta_{c(i)}\), the regressor sees one scale, the anchor's, and only biology and noise remain to explain. The calibration uncertainty can be carried along: \(s_{c}^2\), the variance of the cohort offset, adds to the noise of every target in cohort \(c\), so weights such as \(1/(\sigma_c^2 + s_c^2)\) let well-calibrated cohorts count more. The paper evaluates the classification endpoint; for regression the same argument holds, but we report no regression results here.

4.3Why not correct the features, or use a bigger model?

  • The shift is in the labels, not in the features. Batch corrections such as ComBat act on the features. Applied to the phenotype as a per-cohort mean-centering, they remove every difference between cohort means, including the real biological ones when cohorts sample different populations, and over-correct. phenocal estimates the offset only from isolates whose biology is held fixed by the genome.
  • Model capacity does not replace calibration. In the paper's simulations, larger neural models trained on uncalibrated labels do no better than logistic regression, while calibration improves every learner tested.

5Worked example

The toy example is in the repository and runs in a fraction of a second:

phenocal --phenotypes examples/toy_phenotypes.tsv \
         --pairs examples/toy_distance_matrix.tsv \
         --anchor labA --min-support 2 --out toy

Three strains (s1, s2, s3) are measured in three laboratories. Every strain is present in every laboratory, so every pair of cohorts has three pairs at distance 0.

For labA and labB the three log-ratios are all \(\log_{10}(0.5)\), so \(\Delta_{AB} = -0.301\), with \(\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{(0.213^2 + 0.213^2 + 0)/3} = 0.174\). The three edges agree (\(2 \times 2.5 = 5\)), and the solution is \(\delta_B = \log_{10} 2\) and \(\delta_C = \log_{10} 5\). phenocal reports labB 1.99× [1.05, 3.78] and labC 5.01× [2.64, 9.26] against labA: centred on the true factors, and wide because each edge rests on three pairs only. After calibration each strain has the same value in every laboratory (0.50, 1.00, 2.00 within 1%).

The same result comes from the pair table examples/toy_pairs.tsv; the repository tests check that the two inputs give identical outputs. Add --dashboard toy.html for an interactive report of the run, or --trace toy.md for the same calculations written out step by step with the actual numbers.

6Assumptions and limits

  • Identical genomes share their biology. Isolates at small allelic distance are assumed to have the same true phenotype. The adaptive bound τ and the λ·τ term limit the effect of the biology that remains between near-identical isolates; pairs at distance 0 are the cleanest evidence.
  • The laboratory effect is a constant factor. Each cohort scales all its values by one factor (a constant shift on the log scale). Effects that depend on the value itself are not modelled.
  • The comparison graph must be connected through genetically linked isolates. Cohorts that share no clone with any other cohort cannot be calibrated.
What cannot be told apart. A biological difference that affects every isolate of one cohort in the same way, including its clones shared with other cohorts, looks exactly like a protocol offset and would be calibrated away. Biology specific to some strains does not have this problem: it makes the log-ratios grow with genomic distance, which is what the bound τ detects.
